## Preface



Digital value systems today share a foundational assumption:



**A user signs an authorization once, and the system executes it immediately.**



This model is correct for discrete transfers and final settlement. But when extended into programmable finance, it becomes the **central architectural limitation** preventing reliable coordination.



Coordinated financial behavior requires:



- persistent, revisable intent  

- revocation at any moment  

- pacing and throttling  

- bounded, scoped delegation  

- multi-step workflows  

- cross-domain continuity of authorization  



Modern systems cannot express these natively because **consent does not persist** beyond a single execution. Once a signed instruction is consumed, the underlying authorization vanishes.



This paper identifies the **One-Shot Authorization Flaw** as a **structural barrier** that fragments coordination, weakens automation, and forces ecosystems to simulate continuity through external scaffolding rather than derive it from a native primitive.



**Scope Note:**  

This paper analyzes limitations in *authorization semantics*, not base-layer transaction execution. It does not claim that the one-shot model directly causes phenomena such as MEV, liquidations, or bridge failures. It claims that the absence of durable, revocable consent removes the architectural substrate needed to pace, coordinate, or safely automate responses to these phenomena.



RIP-000 establishes the architectural diagnosis that motivates RIP-001, the durable authorization primitive built to address this gap.

## 2. Abstract



Most blockchain systems encode authorization as a **one-shot instruction**:  

a single-use signature that becomes invalid the moment it is executed.



This paradigm is sufficient for transferring value, but fundamentally insufficient for coordinating it.



Coordination requires:



- ongoing authorization  

- scope and rate limits  

- time-bound validity  

- revocation  

- sequencing  

- composability across domains  



Because one-shot authorization cannot express these, ecosystems have constructed scaffolding — bots, keepers, schedulers, relayers, intents solvers — to *approximate* continuity.



These layers function as **emulations of missing primitives**, not replacements for them.



This paper isolates the **One-Shot Authorization Flaw**:  

a foundational limitation that fragments coordination, weakens automation, and restricts what financial systems can safely express.

## 4. Why One-Shot Authorization Cannot Support Coordination



### 4.1 Consent Does Not Persist  

Once consumed, authorization ceases to exist. There is no object representing ongoing permission.



### 4.2 Revocation Is Impossible  

A submitted instruction cannot be withdrawn mid-flow. Systems cannot express “stop” or “pause.”



### 4.3 No Execution Pacing  

Primitive-level authorization does not support:



- throttling  

- gradual liquidation  

- rate-limited rebalancing  

- controlled streaming  



All actions occur at full, instantaneous speed.



### 4.4 Authorization Is Domain-Bound  

Intent must be reconstructed independently in every chain or environment. There is no portable semantics for consent.



### 4.5 Delegation Cannot Be Bounded Natively  

One-shot semantics cannot express:



- time windows  

- budgets  

- partial consent  

- restricted roles  

- conditionally valid authorization  



These must be reimplemented at higher layers.



---



## 5. Emergent Weaknesses



The limitation manifests in predictable architectural symptoms:



### 5.1 Automation Brittleness  

Systems relying on one-shot submissions must repeatedly re-authorize actions, creating fragility.



### 5.2 Fragmented Authorization State  

Every domain maintains its own authorization model. No shared or portable representation of consent exists.



### 5.3 MEV in Automation Systems  


Repeated single-use instructions create timing surfaces exploitable through front-running, sandwich attacks, and order flow manipulation.


### 5.4 Cross-Domain Inconsistency  

Authorization cannot follow users across chains. Portability must be reconstructed instead of inherited.



### 5.5 Inability to Pace Flows  

Critical financial behaviors such as streaming, gradual liquidation and bounded rebalancing cannot be expressed directly because no primitive represents sustained consent.

## 6. Related Work



Existing mechanisms address parts of the problem but do not replace the underlying primitive:



- **ERC-20 approvals**: persistent but overbroad, non-portable, and non-revocable mid-process  

- **ERC-2612 Permit**: stateless approvals, but still one-shot and non-revocable  

- **Permit2**: bounded approvals, but no cross-domain semantics  

- **Account Abstraction (EIP-4337)**: flexible validation, not portable durable consent  

- **Automation networks / keepers**: simulate continuity through repeated transactions  

- **Intents frameworks**: describe goals but require solvers and lack persistent delegated consent  

- **Bridges and messaging layers**: approximate continuity across domains, do not unify authorization  



These systems operate **on top of** one-shot authorization; none replace it.

## 7. Consent: The Missing Primitive



Coordination requires:



- **durable consent**  

- **revocation paths**  

- **portable authorization semantics**  

- **bounded, scoped delegation**  

- **time-aware execution**  

- **pacing controls**  



No such primitive exists in current architectures. This is not an oversight, it's a foundational constraint inherited from Bitcoin's design goals.

As a result, coordination must be emulated, not expressed.



The absence of a persistent authorization object is therefore one of the most significant architectural constraints in digital value systems.

## 8. PPOs: A Replacement Authorization Primitive



The Recur Consent Layer introduces **Permissioned-Pull Objects (PPOs)**;  

durable, portable, revocable representations of bounded consent.



PPOs allow systems to:



- maintain intent across time  

- revoke consent at any moment  

- bound execution by rate, amount, or conditions  

- carry authorization across chains  

- express multi-step workflows natively  

- eliminate reliance on repeated one-shot submissions  



PPOs do not modify settlement semantics. They replace the *authorization model* to enable reliable coordination atop existing chains.

## Glossary



**One-Shot Authorization**  

A signature consumed immediately upon execution, with no persistence.



**Durable Consent**  

Authorization that remains valid until explicitly revoked or expired.



**PPO (Permissioned-Pull Object)**  

A portable, revocable object representing bounded, time-scoped consent.



**Coordination**  

Multi-step, paced, or conditional workflows requiring ongoing authorization.



**Portability**  

The ability for authorization to retain meaning across execution domains.



**Automation Brittleness**  

Fragility arising from repeated one-shot submissions required to simulate continuity.
